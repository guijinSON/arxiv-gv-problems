The solver is handed a prime field and a weight vector, i.e. the weighted projective
space `X = P(w1,w2,w3)` over `F_q`, and must produce three elements of its
β-graded vanishing ideal. `verify` operates on exactly those objects: it evaluates the
submitted binomials in `F_q` at the rational points of `X`, checks their β-degrees in
`Z`, and checks minimality by two-generated numerical-semigroup membership. Delete the
toric structure and there is no problem left.

## What the family is

Fix a prime `q` and pairwise coprime weights `w1, w2, w3`. Grade
`S = F_q[x1,x2,x3]` by `deg(x_i) = w_i`. The β-graded vanishing ideal `I(X(F_q))` of the
weighted projective space is generated by six binomials. Three of them are the "cell of
size two" generators `x_i x_j (x_i^{(q-1)w_j} - x_j^{(q-1)w_i})`, and those are **given
to the solver in the statement**. The other three come from the full cell
`ε = {1,2,3}` of Theorem 4.5 and have the shape

```
f_i = x1*x2*x3*( x_i^{E_i} - x_j^{F_ij} * x_k^{F_ik} ),   {i,j,k} = {1,2,3}, j < k
```

The solver must write down `f_1, f_2, f_3`, where the exponents are integers ≥ 1 with

1. β-homogeneity: `E_i·w_i = F_ij·w_j + F_ik·w_k`;
2. vanishing on `X(F_q)`;
3. `E_i` is the **smallest** positive integer admitting such `F_ij, F_ik ≥ 1`.

Checking an answer is cheap and exact: (1) is one integer identity; (2) is evaluation in
`F_q` at the rational points (`_eval_points`); (3) is a sweep `c < E_i/(q-1)` of
two-generated semigroup membership tests, each one modular inverse and one comparison.
No floats anywhere. Conditions (1)–(3) pin the answer down completely —
`enumerate_all` returns **exactly 1** valid answer at every preset, shipping included.

**Which sections.** Section 6.2 (*Weighted Projective Spaces*) fixes the definitions and
the standing gcd hypothesis; Proposition 6.1 (`p:IdealWPS`) gives
`I(X(F_q)) = I(A^3_G(F_q))`; Theorem 4.5 (`t:idealAffine`),
`I(A^r_G) = Σ_ε x^ε · I_{(q-1)L_β(ε)}`, is what produces the `(q-1)` twist and the
squarefree prefactor; and the Proposition immediately after Theorem 6.2 displays these
exact six binomials in the non-symmetric case, stating the property this family verifies
— *"these `a_i`'s are the smallest positive integers with that property"* (citing
Herzog [JH1970]).

## Why it is hard (Track B — the algorithm, named)

**The algorithm that solves it.** Lagrange–Gauss reduction of the rank-2 lattice
`ker[w1 w2 w3] ⊂ Z^3`, followed by a scan of the box `|α|,|β| ≤ 3` of integer
combinations of the reduced basis for the three sign patterns `(+,-,-)`, `(-,+,-)`,
`(-,-,+)`. Complexity `O(log w)`. **Measured at the shipping preset: 684 exact integer
operations, 8.8e-5 s, correct on 200/200 instances across five scales** (`compact_route`).

**Why a model cannot just run the mechanical route instead.**

| route | operations at `hard` | seconds |
|---|---|---|
| compact (lattice reduction) | **622 – 684** | 9.8e-5 |
| semigroup sweep `c = 1..a_i`, modular membership | **1,856,340** | 0.73 |
| same sweep, membership by enumeration (no modular inverse) | **232,925,903,078** | — |
| evaluation-matrix kernel (the standard vanishing-ideal route) | **does not solve it at any cost** | — |

Ratio compact : mechanical = **1 : 2,984**. The compact route's ~700 operations are on
11-digit integers, which is at the edge of what a model can carry in context; 1.9 million
is not.

**The linear-algebra trap, and why this family is not in it.** The reflex attack on any
"vanishing ideal" question is: the polynomials of bounded degree vanishing on a finite
point set are the kernel of an evaluation matrix, so row-reduce once and read them off.
That attack is implemented (`attack_linear_algebra_kernel`) and **measured**, not
dismissed. Two findings:

* Building the `F_q` evaluation matrix of the graded piece at the demo preset
  (13 monomials × 124 points, rank 12, kernel dimension 1) and then testing candidates of
  the asked shape: **392 of 392 lie in the kernel.** Every binomial
  `x1x2x3(x_i^{(q-1)c} - x_j^{(q-1)F} x_k^{(q-1)G})` vanishes identically on `F_q^3`,
  because `t^{q-1} = 1` for `t ≠ 0`. The kernel has *zero* discriminating power: it
  cannot tell the minimal generator from the 391 non-minimal ones.
* Computing the integer kernel lattice `ker[w1 w2 w3]` by one extended-Euclid row
  reduction and submitting its basis rows (plus their negated sum, sign-fixed): **0/8**.
  A row-reduced basis has entries of size `~w` (11 digits); the answer's entries are of
  size `~sqrt(w)` (6 digits).

What actually pins the answer is condition (3), *minimality*, which is a
two-dimensional integer program over that lattice — not a linear condition over `F_q`.
That is the framing this family is built on, and it is what keeps it out of the trap.

**Easy regimes that had to be avoided.** (a) Theorem 6.2 (`t:IdealWPS`) solves
`P(1,…,1,a,b)` in closed form — every cell is a complete intersection and the answer is
read straight off the weights. So no weight is ever 1. (b) If `⟨w1,w2,w3⟩` is *symmetric*
the ideal collapses to two generators and one is trivial. The inverse construction
samples all six off-diagonal exponents ≥ 1, which by Herzog's structure theorem forces
the non-symmetric case with exactly three critical binomials.

## Generation: the answer is planted first

`make_instance` samples the six off-diagonal exponents `a_12, a_13, a_21, a_23, a_31,
a_32 ∈ [1, scale]`, sets `a_1 = a_21 + a_31`, `a_2 = a_12 + a_32`, `a_3 = a_13 + a_23`,
forms the three exponent vectors `m_1 = (a_1,-a_12,-a_13)`, `m_2 = (-a_21,a_2,-a_23)`,
`m_3 = (-a_31,-a_32,a_3)` (which sum to zero), and takes the weight vector to be the
**cross product** `w = m_1 × m_2`. Every `m_i` is then automatically in `ker[w]`. Herzog's
structure theorem for 3-generated non-symmetric numerical semigroups guarantees the
planted `a_i` are the critical ones; this was checked directly against brute force on
every sampled instance during development (0 mismatches / 48 pairwise-coprime samples in
the first sweep, and G1 re-checks it on 24 instances per selftest). The only rejections in
the sampling loop are cheap properties of the *plant* — positivity, pairwise coprimality
(~76% rejected), and uniqueness of the positive factorisation of `a_i·w_i` — never a
solve of the instance.

## Worked example (`demo`, seed 1)

`q = 5`, `w = (6, 7, 11)`. Rendered statement: see `render(inst)`; it is ~55 lines and
fully self-contained. Answer:

```
x1^13*x2*x3 - x1*x2^5*x3^5;  x1*x2^17*x3 - x1^5*x2*x3^9;  x1*x2*x3^13 - x1^9*x2^13*x3
```

`verify(inst, answer) == (True, 'ok')`. Reading off `f_1`: `E_1 = 12 = 3·(q-1)`,
`F_12 = F_13 = 4 = 1·(q-1)`, and `3·6 = 18 = 1·7 + 1·11`. Minimality by hand: `6 ∉ ⟨7,11⟩⁺`,
`12 ∉ ⟨7,11⟩⁺`, `18 = 7 + 11` ✓. `enumerate_all` = 1, `search_space` = 847,
compact route = 399 operations.

Corrupting `f_1` by doubling its exponents (still β-homogeneous, still vanishing):

```
verify -> (False, "binomial 1 is not minimal: E = 24 = 6*(q-1) but the smallest
                   admissible multiplier is 3")
```

**Yes, a person can solve `demo` on paper** — three numbers below 5, checked in a minute.
That is what `demo` is for; `harden.py` skips it.

## Caveats — read this

* **Track B is real and is declared.** A polynomial-time algorithm solves every instance
  in well under a millisecond. The claim is *only* that the compact route (lattice
  reduction, ~700 multi-digit integer operations) is what a no-tool model must find, and
  that the route it will otherwise take costs 1.9e6–2.3e11 operations. If a model can
  carry out extended-Euclid plus Gauss reduction on 11-digit integers in its head, it will
  solve this family. That is the bet, and it is the bet Track B is for.
* **The ~700-operation compact route is close to the 1000 cap.** It is under it, and it is
  measured (684 max over 12 shipping seeds, 746 max over 200 instances up to `scale`=1e6),
  but this is not a twelve-step family. A solver who sees the invariant still has real
  arithmetic to do.
* **`P(guess)` is measured against a sampler that already knows the shape,** the `(q-1)`
  divisibility and β-homogeneity — every draw satisfies conditions (1) and (2), so only
  minimality is left to chance. It is *not* uniform over all exponent triples, which would
  have inflated the number by many orders of magnitude. It does assume the solver samples
  `F_ij` log-uniformly on `[1, w_k]`; a solver with a better prior on where `a_ij` sits
  (it is `Θ(sqrt(w))`, which is guessable) does better than `3.2e-31`, though the magnitude
  ansatz attack — which is exactly that prior, with a ±64 window — still fails 8/8.
* **Vanishing is checked exhaustively over `F_q^3` only for `q ≤ 43`.** Above that,
  `_eval_points` uses `{(x,1,1), (1,y,1), (1,1,z)}` plus 1500 random points. That reduced
  set is *complete for the shape verify has already checked*: with all exponents ≥ 1 the
  binomial vanishes automatically wherever a coordinate is 0, and on `(F_q^*)^3` it
  vanishes iff `(q-1)` divides each of `E, F, G`, which the three axis families already
  force. The exhaustive and reduced tests agree on every corruption tested at `q ≤ 43`.
* **Prime fields only.** The paper allows any prime power `q`; `gvlib` carries no finite
  field module, so `F_q` here is `Z/pZ` for prime `p`. This restricts the instance space,
  not the mathematics — the twist by `q-1` and Theorem 4.5 are identical over `F_{p^e}`.
* **Attacks not tried:** LLL in dimension > 2 (unnecessary — the lattice is rank 2 and
  Gauss reduction is optimal there); a general Gröbner-basis engine on the lattice ideal
  (no CAS available, and it would be strictly slower than the reference algorithm, which
  is the same answer); and any attack that uses the *distribution* of `scale` — a solver
  who guesses that the plant was drawn uniformly from `[1, scale]` and knows `scale` could
  narrow the search, but `scale` is not in the rendered statement and is only recoverable
  from the weights up to the same `sqrt(w)` ambiguity the magnitude ansatz already fails on.
* **`canonical_key` keys on `(q, sorted(weights))`.** The family's only relabelling is the
  `S_3` action on variables, and that is exactly what the key quotients by — verified in
  both directions (invariance on all 6 permutations, and the relabelled instance verifying
  against the carried answer). Two different seeds that happened to produce the same weight
  multiset *would* collide, correctly: they are literally the same problem.
